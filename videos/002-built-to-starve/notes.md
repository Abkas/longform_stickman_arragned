## Working angle

**"Your body was built to starve."** Feast-famine wasn't an occasional emergency for
almost all of human history — it was the default operating mode, and the human body
is a purpose-built survival machine for exactly that pattern: unusually fat compared
to every other primate, wired with a metabolic gear-shift that spares the brain when
food runs out, and (per skeletal evidence) chronically cycling through real nutritional
stress episodes throughout the Paleolithic. Reframe/twist at the end: the same trait
that kept your ancestors alive is arguably now working against you in a world of
constant abundance — direct, controversial hook into the modern
diet-culture/intermittent-fasting conversation, without touching genuinely dark
subject matter.

## Narrative arc for this video

Hook: modern "I'm starving" (skipped lunch) vs. the literal, chronic starvation risk
that shaped the human body for ~2 million years. Pillar 1 establishes humans are
biologically *built* for this — "the fat primate." Pillar 2 explains the internal
machinery (ketosis) that makes weeks-long fasting survivable at all. Pillar 3 proves
with skeletal evidence that this wasn't rare — real ancient people show repeated
starvation-stress episodes written into their bones and teeth. Payoff pivots to direct
physical evidence of the *other* half of the cycle — deliberate fat-gorging behavior,
via Ötzi's actual last meal. Reframe: the "thrifty gene" idea — why a survival
adaptation now plausibly backfires in the modern food environment — landing the
controversial modern-relevance hook.

## Sources

▸ **Pillar 1 — humans are "the fat primate"** — humans carry roughly 14-31% body fat
vs. under 9% in other primates, a genetic/epigenetic shift in how fat cells are
regulated (not just diet) that lets humans bank an unusually large energy reserve.
Framed by researchers as an adaptation to buffer the human brain's very high energy
cost. "What made humans 'the fat primate'?" (Duke University research,
ScienceDaily/Neuroscience News coverage, 2019) https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/06/190626160337.htm
— related: "Metabolic scaling, energy allocation tradeoffs, and the evolution of
humans' unique metabolism" (2024, *PNAS*) https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2409674121

▸ **Pillar 2 — the metabolic switch (ketosis)** — George F. Cahill Jr., "Fuel
Metabolism in Starvation" (2006, *Annual Review of Nutrition*, vol. 26)
https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.nutr.26.061505.111258
— once liver glycogen runs out (about a day in), the body shifts to burning fat and
producing ketone bodies specifically to spare glucose/muscle protein for the brain —
the mechanism that turns "days without food" into "weeks without food" being survivable
at all.

▸ **Pillar 3 — proof this was chronic, not rare** — enamel hypoplasia (thinned enamel
bands) and Harris lines (growth-arrest lines in long bones) are well-established
skeletal markers of periodic childhood nutritional stress, and both show up
repeatedly in Paleolithic hunter-gatherer remains. Specific dated example: the Sunghir
2 and 3 juvenile skeletons (Mid Upper Paleolithic, Russia, ~34,000 years old) show
multiple distinct episodes of enamel hypoplasia and femoral/tibial growth-arrest
lines — general background via reviewed literature, e.g. "Differences in childhood
stress between Neanderthals and early modern humans as reflected by dental enamel
growth disruptions" (2024, *Scientific Reports*)
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-61321-x

▸ **Social payoff — group-level insurance layered on top of individual biology**
(added 2026-08-12 during script drafting, verified via direct search) — Ache
hunter-gatherers (eastern Paraguay) return completely empty-handed on
roughly 40% of hunting days, but bring back very large, highly variable
kills on the days they succeed. Kaplan & Hill, "Food Sharing Among Ache
Foragers: Tests of Explanatory Hypotheses" (1985, *Current Anthropology*,
vol. 26, no. 2) https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/203251 — found
that the largest, highest-variance food items get shared the widest across
camp, read as risk-reduction: pooling individual hunting variance across
the group so no single family's bad luck becomes an actual famine. Used in
the script as the "cultural/social payoff" beat (structure.md step 4),
between the skeletal-stress pillar and the Ötzi payoff.

▸ **Payoff — direct physical evidence of the feast side of the cycle** — Ötzi the
Iceman (Copper Age, ~5,300 years old, Ötztal Alps): stomach-content analysis shows his
last meal was strikingly high in fat, paired with wild ibex/red deer meat and einkorn
cereal — read by researchers as deliberate high-fat "loading" behavior suited to sustained
high-exertion travel. "The Iceman's Last Meal Consisted of Fat, Wild Meat, and Cereals"
(Maixner et al., 2018, *Current Biology*)
https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)30703-6

▸ **Reframe — the "thrifty gene" and the modern mismatch** — James V. Neel, "Diabetes
Mellitus: A 'Thrifty' Genotype Rendered Detrimental by 'Progress'?" (1962, *American
Journal of Human Genetics*, 14(4):353-362) — foundational proposal that genes favoring
efficient fat storage during feast-famine cycles became a liability once food scarcity
disappeared, linking ancient survival biology directly to the modern
obesity/type-2-diabetes conversation. **Honesty flag for the script:** this is a
famous but genuinely contested hypothesis, not settled consensus — more recent work
folds it into a broader "evolutionary mismatch" framing rather than accepting it at
face value. Cite it as an influential, debated idea, not a proven mechanism: "Integrating
the Thrifty Genotype and Evolutionary Mismatch Hypotheses to understand variation in
cardiometabolic disease risk" (2024, *Evolution, Medicine, and Public Health*)
https://academic.oup.com/emph/article/12/1/214/7724648

## Reframe / closing thesis (draft)

Feast-famine wasn't a crisis ancient humans occasionally survived — it was the
baseline condition their entire biology was built around: an unusually fat body as a
buffer, an internal fuel-switching system to stretch that buffer for weeks, and a
brain and gut tuned to gorge hard when food showed up. The skeletal record shows this
cycle repeating generation after generation. The twist: that same finely-tuned
survival machine, dropped into a world where food never runs out, may be part of why
modern bodies struggle the way they do — not a flaw, the same feature running in an
environment it was never built for.

## Risk/caveat notes

- The thrifty gene hypothesis is scientifically contested — script needs to present it
  as "an influential idea that explains a lot, though researchers still argue about how
  much" rather than flat fact, to protect the channel's research-backed credibility.
- Ötzi is Copper Age (~5,300 years ago), not deep Paleolithic — used here as direct
  physical/forensic evidence of feast behavior, not as the video's main time period;
  script should keep the framing clear that this is one data point layered onto a
  much older evolutionary pattern established over ~2 million years.
