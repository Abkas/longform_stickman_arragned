*[flatly]* Somewhere between one and five out of every hundred people you've ever met has measurable psychopathic traits. Not "kind of a jerk at parties." Clinically, checklist-scoring psychopathic.

Evolution is supposed to delete this kind of thing. A trait that hurts everyone around it and helps basically nobody should get quietly erased over a few thousand generations — the same way a bad mutation just stops showing up. And yet the number hasn't moved. Every population anyone's ever studied has it, at roughly the same rate. Not a social-media problem. Not a capitalism problem. A permanent fixture of being human, apparently since before we were fully human.

So here's the actual question : if evolution is this good at deleting things that don't help, why is this one still here? Short answer — it's not a glitch. It's a cheat code. And cheat codes only work if almost nobody else is using them. We're going to walk through the math that keeps it rare, the gene that got way more hype than the data supports, the alarm system your own brain runs to catch it, and why, of all places, corner offices might have more of it than anywhere else on the planet.

One thing first, because the internet will absolutely get this wrong: most people with psychopathic traits are never violent, never criminal, and you've probably worked with, dated, or shared a group project with one without incident. This isn't a true-crime video. Nobody's getting arrested in act three. This is a "why does evolution let this exist at all" video, and the real answer is weirder than a horror story.

And "without incident" is the whole hinge this video swings on. There are two very different kinds of people running this strategy: the ones who get caught — prison, headlines, case studies — and the ones who never do, because the exact same traits that make someone dangerous also make them, under the right lighting, unusually charming and good at telling a room exactly what it wants to hear. Nobody's keeping stats on the second group. That's the entire point of being in it.

Here's the mechanism, and it's basically a cheat code smuggled into game theory. Picture a small group where everyone shares food, backs each other up, plays fair — cooperation as the entire survival plan. Now drop in one guy who takes what's shared and never pays it back. As long as he's the only one doing it, he wins constantly. Free lunch, paid for entirely by everyone else's decency.

Here's the catch that turns this from a vibe into actual math: it only works while it's rare. Get too many free-riders and there's no cooperation left to exploit — the whole thing collapses, cheaters included. So you never get a population of all cooperators. You never get one of all cheaters either. You get a fixed, stable percentage, forever. Not a failure to fix the problem. A solved equation nobody particularly wanted the answer to.

Now picture where this math was actually running for most of human history: not a city, not a company, a band of maybe thirty to fifty people who all hunt, forage, and sleep within shouting distance of each other, for life. Everyone knows everyone. Everyone's reputation is common knowledge by dinner. That's an absolutely brutal environment for a cheater to operate in long-term — and also exactly the environment that guarantees a cheater strategy, if it can survive at all, will survive as a rare specialist, not a majority approach. Too small a group and a cheat gets spotted immediately. Too cooperative a group and there's nothing to exploit. The math only balances at a small, steady trickle — which, weirdly, is precisely the number every modern population still lands on.

This isn't just a nice story, either — it's been tested, directly. Decades ago, computer programs from strategists around the world went head-to-head across hundreds of rounds of a classic cooperation game, every program trying to out-score every other program. The most ruthless, always-betray strategies didn't win. The naive, always-cooperate ones got exploited constantly and lost worse. The strategy that actually won, repeatedly, was almost embarrassingly simple: cooperate first, then just copy whatever the other guy did last time. Nice enough to reward cooperation, ruthless enough to punish a cheater instantly, forgiving enough to let a reformed one back in. That's the actual mathematical environment any real cheating strategy has to survive inside.

*[wry]* And somewhere in California, there's a lizard running this exact strategy with its throat color.

Three color morphs, three totally different game plans. Orange males are bullies who hold huge territory by force. Blue males hold smaller territory and team up with their neighbors. Yellow males don't bother holding territory at all — they just sneak in while the other two are busy fighting. Orange beats blue by brute force. Blue beats yellow by ganging up on sneaks. Yellow beats orange by slipping past a guy too busy posturing to notice the guy stealing his girlfriend. Whichever color gets too common becomes the easy target, so the population just cycles forever — real-life rock-paper-scissors that nobody ever actually wins. Nobody designed this. It's just what the math looks like when a cheat strategy is genuinely viable. Same logic, same rare human free-rider, just better camouflage and worse rent.

Translation, back to humans: evolution didn't fail to delete the cheaters. It ran the actual math, and the answer that came back was "keep a small, stable percentage of them, forever."

*[deadpan]* Okay — so if this is baked into the actual math, there should be a gene for it somewhere. Right? A big red "psychopath" button in the DNA.

This is where headlines got way, way ahead of the actual finding, so buckle up for some myth-busting.

There's a real gene people nicknamed — and I promise I'm not making this up — the "warrior gene." People with a low-activity version of it, provoked in a lab economic game, hit back noticeably harder than everyone else. Real result, real data, published in a real journal. And the internet did exactly what the internet does with a gene that has a cool nickname: it ran wild with it. "SCIENTISTS FIND THE PSYCHOPATH GENE." Except a much older, much more careful, decades-long study tracking hundreds of boys into adulthood tells a completely different story: that gene variant only predicted later violence in boys who had also lived through a genuinely brutal childhood. Boys with the same gene and a stable, non-abusive upbringing showed no elevated risk at all. Same gene. Two totally different outcomes, depending entirely on what happened to the kid holding it.

*[deadpan]* So the honest, unflattering answer to "is there a psychopath gene" is: there's a real gene, there's a real gene-times-childhood interaction that keeps replicating, and no — you cannot cheek-swab your annoying coworker and get a verdict. The pop-science version flattens a genuinely careful finding into one scary word that fits in a headline. The real finding is smaller, weirder, and, annoyingly, more interesting: this strategy isn't stamped in at birth, fully formed. It gets assembled, one bad break at a time, out of genetics colliding with circumstance — which is exactly the messy, probabilistic process a rare-cheater strategy needs to keep running at all.

Here's where it gets better than a gene, though, because if evolution keeps a cheat strategy around forever, it should also build an alarm system specifically for it. It did. And you can catch it running, live, in your own head, right now.

Give most people an abstract logic puzzle and they bomb it — genuinely, reliably bad at it. Rewrite the exact same puzzle as "catch this person breaking a promise," and accuracy jumps off a cliff, in every culture ever tested for it, including groups with essentially zero exposure to Western logic puzzles. Same underlying problem. Wildly different brain, the instant it smells a cheater. There's a real, ongoing academic argument over whether this means you've got one dedicated "cheater-detection organ" sitting in your skull specifically, or something broader that just gets sharper whenever a rule feels personally relevant — but either way, it's real, it's replicated, and it means you are, right now, carrying specialized wiring whose entire job is catching the people this strategy lets slip through. An arms race that's never ended, because neither side was ever supposed to fully win.

*[wry]* You've actually felt this exact circuit turn on. It's the specific flavor of suspicion that hits when a friend cancels plans for the third time with a slightly-too-detailed excuse — not annoyance, suspicion, a very particular mental gear shifting from "take this at face value" to "cross-reference this against everything he's said before." That gear doesn't turn on for a math problem. It turns on, hard and fast, only when the question becomes "is this specific person lying to me." That's not a coincidence. That's the alarm system doing exactly the one job it was built for.

And "how many are there, really" turns out to be a genuinely disputed number, not a clean one. Depending how strictly you measure it, the number moves — call it one in a hundred on the strict end, climbing toward four or five in a hundred on the loosest end. That's not "nobody knows anything." That's just an honest number that comes with a range instead of a suspiciously precise decimal point.

*[flatly]* And worth saying plainly, not just implying: even at the high end of that range, that's still ninety-five-plus out of every hundred people you'll ever meet who are not running this strategy at all. This isn't a video about being afraid of strangers. It's a video about a small, stable, mathematically-required minority — small enough that "everyone's a little psychopathic" is exactly the confidently wrong take this whole video exists to correct, not confirm.

*[wry]* So if it's genuinely rare, genuinely disputed, and mostly non-violent — where does it actually show up in the wild, if not in a true-crime documentary?

Think about the laziest person in every group project you've ever been part of — the one who somehow always ends up presenting the slides someone else made, nodding along in meetings, taking exactly as much credit as the room will allow and not one ounce more. Most of the time that's just a lazy classmate. But zoom out on the pattern, and you're looking at a live, low-stakes demo of the exact same free-rider math running in miniature. Small group, shared effort, one person quietly not paying his share of the cost. The group's too big and too transient for anyone to fully catch him, and that's precisely the condition the math needs.

Now scale that same demo up by about a thousand people and give it a corner office.

Here's the part that gets personal fast. In the general population, the strict rate sits around one percent. In corporate leadership specifically, it runs close to three times that.

There's an entire real body of research built around exactly why. The short version: the modern office — fast-moving, high-reward, thin on real accountability, promotions decided in five-minute interviews by people who'll never actually work beside you — is an unusually good habitat for this exact strategy. Confident, charming, great at taking credit, genuinely unbothered by who gets thrown under the bus along the way. In a forty-person hunter-gatherer band, that guy gets caught in a week, because everyone is watching everyone, all the time, with nothing else to do. In a company with six layers of management between him and the person actually doing the work? The alarm system evolution built for a forty-person band never had to evolve for a forty-thousand-person org chart. The strategy didn't go extinct when we invented elevators. It just found a much bigger habitat where the old warning signs stopped working, and it can run the exact same play at a scale a Pleistocene brain was never built to monitor.

Think about what a job interview actually rewards. Thirty minutes of unverifiable claims, delivered confidently, to a stranger who has no access to your actual track record and every incentive to trust the version of you that sounds most impressive. That's not a security flaw in the hiring process. That's the hiring process, working as designed, tuned by decades of "hire for confidence" advice — and it happens to be the exact test a rare free-rider strategy is built from the ground up to ace. The forty-person band never had interviews. It had years of firsthand observation. The org chart swapped years of observation for thirty minutes of vibes, and the math noticed immediately.

*[wry]* Which is exactly why there's now an actual corporate screening test built specifically to catch this before the promotion, not after the damage — cheater-detection with a company logo on it, because the gut instinct alone stopped being enough the moment the "band" got too big to watch.

So — did getting rid of psychopaths, so to speak, ever actually stand a chance?

Here's the reframe, and it's the whole point of the video: evolution didn't lose this fight. It never entered it. Deleting a trait only makes sense when that trait is a straightforward liability every time, for everyone, in every context — a bad mutation with no redeeming math. This isn't that. This is a strategy that only works by staying a minority, propped up by the exact cooperative majority it quietly exploits, kept in permanent check by a detection system running in every honest brain around it. That's not a design flaw sitting in the human genome for three hundred thousand years by accident. That's two strategies locked in a stalemate so stable it looks, from the outside, exactly like nothing happening at all.

*[flatly]* Which means the actual unsettling twist isn't "psychopaths are secretly everywhere." It's the opposite. The system is working exactly as designed. The rate never drops to zero because a rate of zero was never the goal — a permanent, tiny, mathematically-necessary minority was. Every population, every era, roughly the same number, forever, not despite evolution's best efforts, but because of them.

So next time someone tells you evolution should have "fixed" this by now — it did. You're just looking at the fixed version.
